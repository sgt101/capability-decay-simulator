Capability Decay Simulator

A specification for a minimal agent-based model of how reliance on AI erodes the human expertise that AI itself depends on — and how that erosion quietly narrows a system's ability to invent, adapt, and teach.

Core thesis & modeling insight

The simulator tests a specific claim: capability in a system originally comes from humans inventing and refining mechanisms under pressure. AI and agents encode the output of that process, but not the process itself. When humans lean on AI instead of building expertise, two things degrade at once — their own capacity to invent new responses when conditions change, and the pool of expertise other humans could have learned from.

Load-bearing distinction

Every human carries two numbers, not one: latent expertise (E) — what they actually know, earned through practice, which is what decays, grows, and transfers peer-to-peer — and observed capability (C) — what their output looks like to an institution, inflated by AI assistance when it's switched on. Institutions judge performance by C. Peer learning, institutional quality, and the model's brittleness are all driven by E. Without this split, "AI raises expertise" and "reliance on AI causes decay" would contradict each other inside the same variable. With it, the system can look more capable while quietly becoming less able to adapt — which is the whole point of building this.

Entities & state

Humans

  • Ei(t) — latent expertise, [0, 1]
  • Ci(t) — observed capability, [0, 1], equals Ei when AI is off
  • Li — individual learning-rate multiplier, drawn once at entry, fixed for the human's tenure
  • ji(t) — current institution

Institutions

  • a node in the institution graph, with a fixed structural prestige derived from its graph degree at creation
  • nj(t) — current population (emergent, not a parameter)
  • Ēj(t) — mean latent expertise of current members (emergent)

Graph

A sparse graph over institutions, generated once at t=0 by preferential attachment (Barabási–Albert). This is what produces the requested shape without a size parameter: a few hub institutions accumulate most of the connections and, because prestige in the mobility model is tied to degree, most of the top-tier humans — while the rest sit on the periphery with thin connectivity and thin access to expertise.

Grouped by concern. Eight are the ones worth changing between runs (N, M, β, δ, ρ, ai_enabled, ai_response_mode, λ) — the rest have defaults that shouldn't need touching for a first experiment.

Initial expertise distribution (t=0 population only)

Latent expertise for the population the simulation starts with is drawn from a skew-normal, clipped to [0, 1], chosen so that the tails hold the extremes — a thin sliver of true novices and a thin sliver of deep experts — while the skew keeps total naivety rare rather than common. ai_response_mode and κ govern visible capability only (§8) — they never touch E. γbelow/γabove and μatr are what actually move E, and they're split deliberately: γ only ever slows growth (the learning a human would have had, but didn't); μatr only ever speeds decay (the expertise a human had, but is losing). Collapsing these into one dampening factor — as an earlier version of this spec did — makes AI reliance merely slow a human's trajectory in both directions at once, including protecting them from decay. That reads as stagnation, not erosion. Keeping them separate is what lets μatr alone answer "how much does AI reliance obliterate expertise a human already has," independent of how much it also suppresses further learning.

Initialization

  1. Build the institution graph with Barabási–Albert(M, m). Each institution's prestige is fixed at creation as its normalized degree.
  2. Create N humans. Assign each to an institution uniformly at random.
  3. Draw each human's Ei(0) from SkewNormal(μE, ωE, αE), clipped to [0,1].
  4. Draw each human's fixed Li ~ Lognormal(0, σL).
  5. Set Ci(0) = Ei(0) (AI mechanism applied from tick 1 onward if enabled).
  6. Fix two benchmarks from this t=0 population, used for the whole run rather than recomputed live: start_top_e = max(Ei) across everyone (anchors ai_level, §8), and start_ebar[j] = mean(Ei) within each institution j (anchors μamb ambient growth, §11) — both fixed specifically so the thing they scale can't feed back into itself as the population evolves.

Per-tick update algorithm

Runs in this order every tick, so that mobility decisions use the same institution averages that the learning step just produced:

# 1. Institution stats (latent, not observed — see §8)
for j in institutions:
    n[j]  = count(members(j))
    Ē[j]  = mean(E[i] for i in members(j))

# 2. AI level, if enabled — fixed at t=0, not recomputed from the current population
ai_level = λ * start_top_e   if ai_enabled else None
# start_top_e = max(E[i] at t=0), set once during initialization

# 3. Learning / decay — growth and decay are dampened independently (see §8)
for i in humans:
    j = institution(i)
    gap   = Ē[j] - E[i]
    below = ai_enabled and E[i] < ai_level

    if gap > 0:                              # peers are stronger — learning
        delta = β * gap * L[i]
        if ai_enabled: delta *= γ_below if below else γ_above
    else:                                    # peers are weaker, or idle — decay, offset by ambient growth
        decay = δ * gap * L[i]
        if below: decay *= μ_atr          # AI reliance actively accelerates this
        ambient = μ_amb * start_ebar[j] * L[i] * (1 - E[i])  # NOT AI-gated — see §11
        delta = decay + ambient

    E[i] = clip(E[i] + delta, 0, 1)
    C[i] = clip(E[i] + κ * boost(E[i], ai_level, ai_response_mode), 0, 1) if ai_enabled else E[i]
    # boost(...) — five interchangeable shapes, see §8

# 4. Mobility — see §6 for candidate-set rules per mode
for i in humans:
    if random() < p_move * L[i]:
        candidates = candidate_institutions(i, mobility_mode)
        i moves to argmax_j utility(i, j)   # softmax-sampled, not deterministic

# 5. Turnover
leaving = sample_uniform(humans, r * N)
remove(leaving)
add r * N new humans, initialized per §4, placed uniformly at random

Institution graph & mobility

mobility_mode is a single categorical parameter — a run can use any of the three; this is the knob for testing whether the graph is load-bearing or just cosmetic.

ModeCandidate setWhat it's for
unconstrainedevery institutionisolates the pure attractiveness dynamic; graph only shapes prestige, not reachability
edge_constrainedgraph neighbors of the current institution onlythe graph is a literal career-pathway map; periphery institutions are genuinely hard to leave
hybridneighbors, with probability ε of considering the full set insteadmostly local mobility with occasional long jumps; default

Utility function

When human i evaluates institution j:

growth(i,j)  = max(0, Ē[j] - E[i])              # room to learn there
status(i,j)  = E[i] - Ē[j]                       # relative standing if i moved there
prestige(j)  = normalized_degree(j)               # fixed, structural

U(i,j) = (1-ρ) * growth(i,j) + ρ * status(i,j) + η * prestige(j)

This is what produces the requested split without extra machinery: mid-expertise humans chase growth toward stronger institutions; once someone is above most institutions' averages, growth drops out and status pulls them toward smaller, lower-competition institutions where they stand out — except at the very top, where prestige keeps the highest performers anchored to the hub institutions even though every other institution would offer more relative standing.

Entrant renewal

The initial-expertise distribution in §3 describes the population the simulation starts with — already a mix of skill levels, as an established system would be. It says nothing about who shows up afterward. Every turnover replacement (§3, "Turnover") draws from a separate, much lower distribution instead: entrants are genuine novices, not miniature copies of the existing population.

SymbolNameMeaningDefault
μententrant_expertise_meanlocation of the entrant skew-normal — deliberately low0.05
ωententrant_expertise_spreadscale, pre-clip0.05
αententrant_expertise_skewshape — unskewed by default; entrants aren't expected to already have a tail of high performers0

This is what makes AI-driven decline compounding rather than a one-time step down to a lower plateau. An entrant only becomes expert by learning from stronger peers — that's the whole point of β (transfer_rate). If AI dampens that transfer (§8, γbelow), entrants stay stuck near zero instead of climbing. And because Ēj is a plain average over an institution's current members, every stuck entrant also drags that institution's average down for everyone else — including existing experts, whose own gap (Ēj − Ei) turns more negative as a result, accelerating their decay too. Under sustained AI reliance the system doesn't just fail to produce new experts; it actively erodes the ones it already has, because the peer pool everyone measures their own gap against is shrinking in quality from both directions at once.

This required retuning transfer_rate, not just adding a parameter

With entrants starting near zero, β's old default (0.02) turned out to be too weak to keep even a no-AI system healthy — turnover injects fresh novices faster than that rate can raise them, so the baseline collapsed right alongside the AI-on case, which defeats the entire comparison. β's default moved to 0.5 specifically so the no-AI counterfactual demonstrates what it's supposed to: a system that can sustain real expertise through peer transfer, against which AI's failure to do the same is the actual finding. At the old default, both arms failed for a reason that had nothing to do with AI — a turnover/transfer mismatch, not a signal. (This also means turnover_rate's useful range shifted — see the note on the batch tooling's experiment set, which is being recalibrated against this mechanism.)

Verified, not assumed: with the new default, a no-AI run reaches and holds roughly 95% of the population above the expert threshold within a couple hundred ticks and stays there for thousands more. Under AI (default γbelow=0.3), the expert share collapses to zero within roughly 200 ticks and never recovers — not a lower plateau, a fully cut-off pipeline — while meanE keeps sliding for as long as it's been tested (several thousand ticks), rather than settling.

AI mechanism

Toggling ai_enabled activates ai_level = λ · start_top_e, where start_top_e is the single highest E in the population at t=0 — "the greatest human expert at the start." It's set once, during initialization, and stays fixed for the entire run; it does not track whoever currently happens to be the top performer, which would make it a moving target that chases the population's own best outcomes rather than a stable benchmark. (Two earlier versions of this spec anchored it differently — first to the live top human, then to the t=0 population's median — see the design-decisions callout in §11 for why each changed.) That level feeds two independent effects: how much AI actively erodes real expertise (below, and the primary thing this model is for), and how much visible boost each human gets (further down — a separate, secondary question about the illusion of capability, not the substance of it).

How AI erodes real expertise

A human below ai_level is, by definition, one AI is substituting for. That reliance does two things to their trajectory, and the spec keeps them as two separate dials on purpose (see §3, AI table):

  • Growth is crowded out. When a stronger institution would have taught them something, γbelow dampens how much of that they actually absorb — the learning that doesn't happen because AI did the task instead.
  • Decay is accelerated. When they're not being taught — idle relative to their institution, or in a weak one — μatr (ai_atrophy_multiplier) speeds up how fast what they already knew erodes. This is the "used it or lost it" half, and before this parameter existed the model had no way to represent it at all: dampening a single signed delta merely slowed decay too, which reads as protection from erosion, not erosion.

The number to look at is shareExpert(t) — the fraction of the population with E ≥ 0.585 (expert_threshold, §11) — compared against a same-seed run with ai_enabled: false. A single AI-on run's meanE means little on its own; the shortfall against that no-AI counterfactual is the actual claim being tested. batch_run.js's pairWithBaseline mode runs both arms with a matched seed automatically — see its README.

What obliteration looks like here

It's a widening shortfall, not a cliff. With the defaults (μatr=1.5), a paired baseline/AI comparison at N=600 shows the population's real expertise shortfall grow from roughly 0.047 at t=100 to 0.076 at t=600 — and the share of the population still reaching expert level (E≥0.7) drops from about 7.7% in the no-AI counterfactual to 5.0% under AI, over the same window. Raising μatr widens both numbers further; it's the one dial most directly answering "how much does reliance on AI obliterate expertise."

Who benefits from the visible boost, and how (secondary)

This part is about C, not E — it never feeds back into anything real. It answers a different, narrower question: given that AI makes output look better, whose output benefits most? Useful for studying the illusion; not where the erosion story lives.

The obvious default — an equalizing floor, where the least expert gain the most because everyone is lifted to the same line — is only one hypothesis. The more commonly claimed effect is the opposite: expertise and AI benefit compound, because a stronger human knows what to ask for and how to judge the answer. ai_response_mode makes that a variable instead of an assumption, and κ (ai_gain) is one shared dial for how strongly whichever shape is selected applies — κ=0 always means "AI enabled but invisible," in every mode.

Each mode is a boost(E) added to expertise: C = clip(E + κ · boost(E), 0, 1).

Modeboost(E)Who benefits
floormax(0, ai_level − E)only humans below ai_level — a ceiling, not a multiplier. At κ=1 this is C = max(E, ai_level), the model's original default.
flatai_leveleveryone, by the same absolute amount — the least expert gain the most in relative terms even though the boost itself doesn't look at E.
linearai_level · Eproportional to expertise — an expert's absolute gain grows in a straight line with how much they already know.
amplifiedai_level · E²superlinear — the multiplier on expertise itself grows with E, so gains accelerate for the already-strong.
exponentialE · (eai_level·E − 1)compounding — the sharpest "rich get richer" shape; at high κ, expert output saturates at the 1.0 ceiling fastest of all five.

    What this does to the brittleness gap

    Under floor, only humans below ai_level carry a C − E gap — the top performers show no boost at all, so the brittleness gap concentrates entirely at the bottom. Under linear, amplified, or exponential, the gap opens everywhere, and opens widest exactly where the real experts are — meaning the humans whose judgment the rest of the system implicitly trusts most are also the ones whose observed output is least representative of what they'd produce unassisted. That reversal is the interesting comparison this parameter is for.

    Why institution quality uses E, not C

    Ēj — the number that drives peer learning and institutional attractiveness — is computed from latent expertise only, in every mode. If it used observed capability instead, AI would make every institution look expert-staffed, the transfer/decay rule would stop discriminating between real and assisted institutions, and the entire brittleness mechanic would disappear. Keeping the two metrics separate is what lets the simulator show a system that looks fine on C while its E — the thing that actually generates new responses when conditions change — quietly flattens.

    Metrics & logging

    Recorded every tick, at both levels, to drive the visualizer without re-deriving anything from raw state:

    • Per institution: nj, Ēj, mean Cj, prestige
    • Population: mean / median / 10th & 90th percentile of E and of C
    • Share expert: fraction of the population with E ≥ 0.585 (expert_threshold, §11) — the headline series; compare against a same-seed AI-off run to see how much AI reliance has obliterated
    • Center–periphery divergence: variance (or Gini) of Ēj across institutions, weighted by degree vs. unweighted
    • Illusion gap (secondary): mean(C) − mean(E) — near zero with AI off, opens up with AI on; measures the appearance of capability, not its erosion
    • Share of humans below ai_level, when AI is enabled

    Visualizer specification

    A simple, single-run visualizer — not a dashboard — built around one idea: let the viewer watch real expertise erode while the network looks unchanged.

    Views

    • Network panel — force-directed layout of the institution graph. Node size = nj, node color = Ēj on a low→high scale. Redraws each tick; hub institutions should visibly hold their position.
    • Distribution panel — animated histogram of E across the population, with a thin overlay line for C when AI is on, so the gap between "real" and "observed" is visible directly, not just as a summary number.
    • Timeline panel — share-expert and center–periphery divergence plotted over t (the illusion gap is available too, but secondary), with a marker at the tick AI was toggled.
    • Institution inspector — click a node to see that institution's member expertise histogram and its history of Ēj.

    Key design decisions

    Flagged because they're interpretive choices made to keep the model coherent and minimal, not things explicitly dictated in the request — worth pushback if any read wrong.

    • Latent vs. observed split (§1). Necessary for "AI raises expertise" and "reliance on AI causes decay" to both be true without contradiction.
    • Barabási–Albert graph. Chosen specifically because it produces emergent hubs from one parameter (m) — matching "largest and most successful institutions" without a separate size input.
    • Symmetric transfer/decay rule, asymmetric AI treatment of it. Growth and decay are still one regression-to-institution-mean formula with two rate constants (β, δ) when AI is off. But when AI is on, the two branches are no longer dampened by the same factor: an earlier version of this spec applied γbelow to whichever branch fired, which meant it slowed decay as much as growth — protecting AI-reliant humans from erosion instead of causing it. μatr (§8) exists specifically to fix that: growth is dampened, decay is separately and independently accelerated.
    • Prestige as a fixed structural term. Without it, the status term alone would push even the top human toward the emptiest institution, contradicting "the highest expertise humans will stay in the highest expertise institutions."
    • Uniform-random exit. Simplest turnover mechanism that satisfies "leave at the same rate"; tenure-based exit is a drop-in alternative if career-length effects matter later.
    • Mobility mode as a run-time switch, not three separate models. Per your steer — all three are implemented as one parameter so a single build supports comparison runs.
    • One gain parameter (κ) shared across five AI response modes. Per your steer on expertise-dependent AI benefit — floor, flat, linear, amplified, and exponential are all boost(E) shapes scaled by the same dial, rather than five independently-parameterized mechanisms, so sweeping "does more expertise mean more AI benefit" is a two-value change (mode, κ), not a model rewrite. floor at κ=1 reproduces the original spec exactly.
    • Entrants are novices, not population clones (§7). Per your steer directly — this was the missing feedback loop. Before it, AI's effect was a lower plateau: turnover kept refeeding the system with reasonably-skilled fresh draws regardless of how bad things had gotten, so decline always leveled off. With genuine novices who can only rise via peer transfer, a suppressed transfer rate means they never rise — and their accumulating presence drags down institution averages for existing experts too. This is what makes the decline actually compounding rather than a one-time adjustment. It forced β's default up 25x (0.02 → 0.5) — otherwise even the no-AI arm failed for reasons unrelated to AI, which would've made the whole comparison meaningless.
    • ai_level fixed to the t=0 median, not the current top performer (§8) — later superseded, see next. Per your steer directly. The original formula (λ · maxi Ei(t)) made the "below AI level" cutoff a moving target: as the top performer's own expertise grew or shrank over the run, the threshold chased it, so the below/above split at any given tick depended on the population's own trajectory rather than a stable external benchmark. Pinning it to the median of the starting population — "the level of the majority of the human population at the start" — fixed it as a constant for the whole run instead, set once during initialization. Checked, not assumed: the self-renewal/collapse contrast this model exists to show (no-AI reaching ~95% expert share, AI-dampened collapsing to 0%) reproduced essentially unchanged under this formula, so it was a definitional correction, not a retune. This was itself later replaced — see the next entry.
    • ai_level anchor changed again: from the t=0 median to the t=0 single greatest expert (§8). Per your steer directly — the concept "λ = fraction of the greatest human expert" (not the majority/median) is what the parameter was meant to capture. Same "fixed, not live" fix as before carries over unchanged: start_top_e is captured once at init from the t=0 population's single highest E, not recomputed from whoever is currently on top, so it doesn't reintroduce the moving-target problem the median anchor was originally built to avoid. Worth knowing if you're reading a specific run: for typical population sizes the t=0 maximum sits very close to the [0,1] ceiling (the skew-normal's own tail routinely produces a near-1.0 draw), so ai_level under this anchor is now much higher, relative to the population, than it was under the median anchor — at the default λ=0.70 it lands around 0.70, rather than deep in the middle of a typical population as before. (At the time this change was made, expert_threshold also happened to be 0.7, making ai_level and expert_threshold numerically coincide under defaults — that coincidence no longer holds now that expert_threshold has moved to 0.585, see the next entry.) That shifts what "below ai_level" means: it now tracks closer to "not yet expert" than to "below the typical human," which changes the practical population split even though the formula's shape (a fixed fraction of a fixed t=0 benchmark) is unchanged.
    • expert_threshold moved from 0.7 to 0.585. Per your steer directly, following an empirical sensitivity check (expert_threshold_sensitivity.js, and "Expert threshold" in README.md). expert_threshold is a bare constant, not a configurable parameter — it only defines share_expert(t) (§9), it never touches the dynamics. The self-renewal/collapse contrast reads cleanly for any threshold roughly in [0.3, 0.86]; outside that band it breaks down at both ends, for different reasons — below ~0.3 the bar is too low to discriminate (even the AI-collapsed population mostly clears it), above ~0.86 the bar exceeds the no-AI baseline's own steady-state ceiling ("Simulation steady state" in paper.md: meanE converges to ≈0.87 even under a healthy no-AI baseline), so a too-high threshold makes even a fully self-renewing population look collapsed — and in the AI-amplification regime specifically, a threshold that high manufactures an artificially large apparent AI benefit, since amplified humans get pushed past a ceiling the unassisted baseline can't reach. 0.7 sat close enough to that upper edge (0.16 away) to inflate the amplification-regime numbers somewhat; 0.585 sits near the middle of the robust band (~0.28 from each edge), which removes that artifact without changing the headline collapse story at all — checked directly, both thresholds show baseline ~97-98%, treatment ~0% under the engine's own default dampening (0.30/0.80 — this check doesn't override it, so it isn't the experiment set's worst-case backdrop).
    • Ambient growth (§5), and why it's anchored to a fixed founding average, not the live one. Per your steer directly — decay alone made the "above your institution's ceiling" branch a dead end, which understates how real experts keep improving. The first version scaled ambient growth by the institution's current average, which is a positive-feedback loop: top performers rise → the average they're compared against rises → next tick's ambient term is larger for everyone → the whole population, not just the top, saturates to E=1 within a few thousand ticks regardless of AI, quietly erasing the model's central contrast. Anchoring it to start_ebar[j] (each institution's own t=0 average, fixed for the run — same fix pattern as ai_level above) breaks that loop. It isn't a permanent equilibrium either — given a long enough horizon the population still drifts toward 1 — but checked directly: within the model's standard 1000-tick horizon it stays a gentle effect (a few percentage points) and leaves the AI/no-AI shortfall under worst-case dampening (0.0/0.0 — the experiment set's fixed-dampening default at the time this was checked; it has since moved to a neutral 1.0/1.0, see the entry on that below) essentially untouched (0.524 → 0.492 at the calibrated default), because it isn't AI-gated — it lifts both arms' shared floor rather than favoring either one.
    • generate_experiments.js's fixed aiDampeningBelow/Above default moved from 0.0 (max dampening) to 1.0 (neutral). Per your steer directly, following a direct empirical check: with both pinned at max dampening — the experiment set's original default, itself an earlier explicit steer — share_expert read a flat 0.000 across the entire range of whatever else was being swept, for every one of the experiments that didn't itself study a dampening parameter. Checked with turnover_rate as a concrete example: meanE moved by only 0.025 total across its full range, and share_expert didn't move at all, versus a real, informative range under moderate dampening. A worst-case backdrop was originally chosen deliberately, to stress-test every other parameter against the harshest AI impact — but in practice it meant most of the 55 experiments couldn't show what the swept parameter actually did, on the metric the whole model exists to report. Neutral (1.0/1.0) removes that bias without introducing an opposite one, since it's the point on the dampening scale where AI reliance has exactly zero effect on learning either way. This doesn't fully restore aiLevelFraction's informativeness in its other eight pairings (still symmetric, so the below/above split still mostly doesn't matter for learning) but does surface a smaller real effect through μatr's own non-neutral default (1.5) acting on the decay branch instead — see the comment above aiLevelFraction in generate_experiments.js for the exact numbers.

    Out of scope / future extensions

    Excluded to keep the parameter count down for a first version; each is a plausible follow-up:

    • Shock events — a periodic disruption that only invention (high E, not C) can respond to. This is the most direct test of "brittle in the face of change" and is the natural next spec once the base model is validated.
    • Institution capacity limits (currently unconstrained; attractiveness alone shapes population)
    • Heterogeneous institution domains (all institutions are currently interchangeable except for graph position)
    • Tenure-weighted or skill-weighted exit instead of uniform-random
    • Graph rewiring over time (currently fixed at t=0)
    • Multiple, competing AI systems at different λ levels
    Draft specification — engine and visualizer described here are not yet implemented.
